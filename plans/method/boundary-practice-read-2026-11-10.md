# How high-risk fields hand work over: a read of the sources, 2026-11-10

## Result first

1. All fields share one frame: a prepared sender, a live talk, a written record, a named transfer and a later check.
2. Only the military gives the purpose its own item. Medicine and mission control carry a part. Plants carry state.
3. The best-measured effect is I-PASS: nine hospitals, 10,740 admissions, medical errors down 23% (24.5 to 18.8).
4. One randomised trial of the same tool found no effect on harm (60.4 against 60.4 events per 1,000 days).
5. No field tests in routine that the purpose arrived. One study did: receivers matched the intent in 34% of 32 cases.
6. The check decays first. Receivers read back in 17% of observed hospital handoffs. Senders do not see the gap.

## How to read this page

Each claim has a source key, for example [M1]. The keys are in the last section. Each claim has one label.

- Measured: a number from a study, with the sample and the year.
- Advice: a rule, a doctrine text or a guidance page. No measurement supports the claim here.
- Opinion: a conclusion of an author.
- Weak: the design, the sample or the route to the source limits the claim.

Some sites refused the fetch: PubMed, the FAA, BAILII, GlobalSecurity and the Army publication site. For those sources
the page uses the abstract or a summary that the search returned. The source list marks each source as "opened" or
"summary only".

## 1. Medicine

### What crosses

- Advice. I-PASS fixes five items in this order: Illness severity, Patient summary, Action list, Situation awareness and
  contingency planning, Synthesis by receiver [M1][M7].
- Advice. SBAR fixes four items in this order: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation [M8].
- The purpose has no item of its own in the two tools. The reason for an action is part of the patient summary and of
  the contingency plan.
- Measured. The reasons do not cross well. Incoming and outgoing interns did not agree on the rationale for 60% of the
  handed-over items (Chang 2010, one hospital) [M12].

### The form

- Advice. The Joint Commission asks for a face-to-face handoff, in a place with no interruptions, with a standard tool
  and with training for sender and receiver. Its 2017 alert names I-PASS [M19].
- Advice. I-PASS is a bundle. The bundle has the mnemonic, a written or computer tool, team training, observation by
  faculty and a campaign to keep the practice [M1][M7].
- Advice. Bedside handover moves the nursing handover to the patient, who can hear and correct the handover [M17].

### The check

- Advice. I-PASS: the receiver restates the summary and the actions in the receiver's own words, and asks questions.
  This is the "Synthesis by receiver" [M1].
- Advice. Read-back: the receiver repeats an order or a critical test result word for word.
- Advice. Teach-back: the clinician asks the patient to restate the instructions in the patient's own words [M14][M15].

### The evidence

- Measured. Starmer 2014, New England Journal of Medicine. Nine paediatric residency programmes, 10,740 admissions.
  Medical errors fell 23%, from 24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions (P<0.001). Preventable adverse events fell 30%, from 4.7
  to 3.3 per 100 admissions. Adverse events that were not preventable did not change (3.0 and 2.8, P=0.79). The duration
  of the handoff did not change. Each site compared a period before with a period after. No site was a control at the
  same time [M1].
- Measured, weak. Starmer 2022, Journal of Hospital Medicine. 32 hospitals. Handoff-related adverse events fell 47%:
  major events from 1.7 to 0.9 per person-year, minor events from 17.5 to 9.3. Handoffs with all five items rose from
  20% to 66%. The residents reported the events in surveys. The grant report of the same project gives 42% [M3].
- Measured, no effect. Jorro-Baron 2021, BMJ Quality and Safety. A stepped-wedge randomised trial of I-PASS in six
  paediatric intensive care units in Argentina, 1,465 records. Preventable adverse events were 60.4 per 1,000 days in
  both periods (P=0.99). Compliance with the tool rose. The handover of each patient took longer [M4].
- Measured, no effect on outcomes. An I-PASS programme in orthopaedic surgery improved 8 of 9 quality items at 18
  months. Readmission, infection and delirium did not change [M5].
- Measured. SBAR: a 2018 review found 11 studies, 8 of them before-and-after designs. 8 of 26 patient outcomes improved.
  The authors call the evidence moderate, and strongest for telephone calls [M8].
- Measured. Nursing handover: the 2014 Cochrane review screened 2,178 citations. No study met the criteria. The review
  could not name a best handover style [M9].
- Measured, weak. Bedside handover: one 2021 review reports falls reduced by 9.3% to 80% across studies of different
  designs. A 2024 review of 30 studies reports results that contradict one another and warns of publication bias [M17].
- Measured. A meta-analysis of 36 studies found positive effects of handoff protocols on information passed (g=0.71) and
  on patient outcomes (g=0.53). All 36 studies were before-and-after designs. The authors found significant publication
  bias [M11].
- Measured. Read-back of critical laboratory results by telephone: read-back found 29 errors in 822 calls, a rate of
  3.5% (Barenfanger 2004, three organisations) [M16].
- Measured, weak. Teach-back: a 2020 review found 20 studies of moderate quality. 19 reported a benefit. The settings
  and the outcomes differ too much to pool [M14].
- Measured, weak. Physicians checked the recall of the patient in 12 of 61 visits (20%). Patients whose physician
  checked had better blood sugar control (odds ratio 8.96, 95% interval 1.1 to 74.9). The study was observational, with
  74 patients [M15].

### The known failures

- Measured. Receivers do not do the check. In 48 observed hospitalist handoffs the receiver read back in 8 (17%) and
  took notes in 11 (23%). Nods and "yes" were common [M13].
- Measured, weak. Residents said that they used "Synthesis by receiver" least of the five items. They used all five when
  a person observed them [M7].
- Measured. The sender does not know that the handoff failed. Interns rated their own handoffs as effective while the
  receivers missed key items [M12].
- Measured. Compliance with the tool can rise while patient harm stays the same [M4][M5].
- A study that follows one handoff programme for years and reports the decay was not found. Short audits at 2 to 6
  months report both held and lost compliance (weak).

### What the field says does not work

- Opinion. Cohen and Hilligoss 2010: the literature does not support a mandate to standardise. "Standardise" has no
  clear meaning. A handoff has more functions than the transfer of data, and a standard can damage those functions
  [M10].
- Opinion. A recorded or written-only report gives the receiver no chance to ask [O1].
- Advice. Passive listening does not help memory. The authors of [M13] ask for active listening and fewer interruptions.

## 2. Aviation

### What crosses

- Advice. Pilot and controller: one clearance or instruction at a time. The reason is not part of the message.
- Advice. Controller relief: a checklist of the facility lists the items in a fixed order. The items are status items,
  such as traffic, weather and equipment [A5].
- Advice. Maintenance shift change: the state of each open task, which steps are done and which parts are removed, in a
  written turnover and in speech [A7][A9].
- Advice. Crew briefing: the plan for the departure or the approach. One airline changed the order and put the threats
  first [A10].

### The form

- Pilot and controller use speech in fixed phrases. ICAO rules list the items that the pilot must always read back, such
  as runway clearances, levels and headings [A2].
- Controller relief has four parts: the incoming controller previews the position, the outgoing controller briefs in
  speech, the incoming controller takes responsibility, and the outgoing controller reviews the position before leaving
  [A5].
- Maintenance uses written work cards and turnover forms, with a spoken turnover [A7].

### The check

- Read-back: the pilot repeats the safety-related parts word for word. Hear-back: the controller must listen to the
  read-back and correct a difference at once. "Roger" and "Wilco" must not replace a read-back [A2].
- Controller relief: the incoming controller states that the incoming controller has the position. Both share the
  responsibility for a complete briefing. The outgoing controller stays and checks that nothing was missed [A5].
- The check tests the words. No rule asks the pilot to state what the clearance is for.

### The evidence

- Measured. Tower tapes, 49 hours, 10 towers (Cardosi 1994). Controllers missed 7 read-back errors. Those were 37% of
  the read-back errors and less than 0.1% of all controller messages. Messages with six or seven items had more errors
  (1.3% to 1.4%) than messages with one to five items (below 1%) [A3].
- Measured. Approach control tapes, 48 hours, 8 facilities. Fewer than 1% of the messages ended in a communication error
  [A3].
- Measured, weak. In incident reports from pilots and controllers (ASRS, 1998), 47% of the communication errors were a
  wrong read-back that the controller did not catch. In 25% the pilot gave no read-back. In 18% the controller did not
  notice the controller's own error in a correct read-back. The reports are voluntary [A4].
- Measured. The first ten minutes after a controller takes a position have the highest share of operational errors of
  all ten-minute periods. This held after years of work on the relief briefing (FAA, 2008) [A6].
- No controlled study of the crew briefing was found. The texts on briefings are advice.

### The known failures, from accident reports

- Tenerife, 27 March 1977, 583 dead. The KLM crew read back a route clearance and added "We are now at takeoff". The
  tower answered "OK ... Stand by for take-off, I will call you". A second transmission at the same moment hid the last
  part. The Spanish report names the take-off without clearance as the cause and the phrases as a factor. After the
  accident, "takeoff" is spoken only in the take-off clearance and its cancellation. "OK" and "Roger" no longer
  acknowledge an instruction. Crew training changed to let junior crew challenge a captain [A1].
- Continental Express 2574, 11 September 1991, 14 dead. The second shift removed 47 screws from the top of the left
  stabiliser edge. The inspector wrote the work on the turnover sheet after the incoming inspector had read the sheet.
  Nobody told the third shift in speech. The third shift closed the task. The NTSB named the failure to follow the
  maintenance procedures and the failure of management to enforce them [A7].
- Airbus A320 G-KMAM, Gatwick, 26 August 1993. Maintenance left four of five right spoilers in maintenance mode after a
  flap change. The status of the spoilers was not briefed across the shift handover. The aircraft flew and returned
  [A8].

### What the field says does not work

- A bare acknowledgement ("OK", "Roger") [A1][A2].
- Long messages. The error rate rises with the number of items [A3].
- A read-back with no listener. The largest error class is the read-back that nobody checks [A4].
- A spoken-only turnover in maintenance, and a written turnover that the receiver reads before it is complete [A7].

## 3. The military

### What crosses

- Advice. The order states the commander's intent. Army doctrine defines the intent as a short statement of the purpose
  of the operation and the desired end state. It can list key tasks [W2].
- Advice. The purpose comes first and has its own paragraph. The order also gives the mission and the intent of the
  headquarters one level and two levels up [W4][W6].
- Advice. The older Army text says that leaders two levels down must understand the intent. It asks for three to five
  sentences that are easy to remember [W4].
- Advice. Marine Corps doctrine gives every mission two parts, the task and the intent. It treats the intent as the part
  that lasts when the situation changes. The exact wording was not opened in this read [W6].
- Advice. Relief in place: the incoming unit takes over the mission and the area. The outgoing unit teaches its local
  methods and its lessons. Detail on the items was not opened (weak) [W7].

### The form

- A fixed five-paragraph order, in writing and in speech, often over a terrain model.
- Relief in place has a period where both units are present. The outgoing unit mentors the incoming unit before the
  transfer of authority [W7].

### The check

- Advice. Confirmation brief: directly after the order, each subordinate leader tells the commander the leader's
  understanding of the intent and of the task [W3].
- Advice. Back-brief: later, each subordinate tells the commander how the subordinate will do the mission. The commander
  corrects the plan against the intent [W3].
- Advice. Rehearsal: the units act out the plan together [W3].
- The check is a restatement in the receiver's own words, and then the receiver's own plan. This is a stronger check
  than a read-back.

### The evidence

- Measured. Shattuck (study reported 2000). Four active battalions. Each commander wrote an order with a statement of
  intent. Sixteen company commanders then met two changed situations each, which gives 32 cases. The battalion
  commanders judged 17 of 32 responses (53%) as a match with their intent. Shattuck removed three chance matches and
  three responses that differed in substance. The result was 11 of 32, or 34% [W1].
- The study tested one level down. No study of two levels down was found.
- No study of the effect of the back-brief or of the confirmation brief was found.

### The known failures

- Measured. Company commanders who failed "generally did not refer to the battalion commander's statement of intent".
  Some followed procedure when the situation was new. Sender and receiver gave different meanings to a doctrine term
  such as "delay" [W1].
- Opinion. The schools teach how to write an intent statement. Officers get almost no practice in how to read one and
  act on it [W1].
- Advice. Lessons texts say that units confuse the back-brief with coordination [W3].

### What the field says does not work

- Long lists of key tasks. The Army removed key tasks from the intent in 2010 because they had become long lists. The
  Army put them back in 2012 [W5].
- A written intent statement alone, with no practice on changed cases [W1].

## 4. Operations that run in shifts for years

### What crosses

- Advice. The state of the plant or the system, the work in progress, the permits, the overrides of safety systems and
  the abnormal conditions [O3][O4][O7].
- Advice. DOE standard for nuclear facilities: a turnover checklist names the logs to read, such as the narrative log,
  the tag-out log and the temporary modification log [O7].
- Measured, by observation. Space shuttle mission control was the only one of four settings where the outgoing person
  gave the team's "stance toward changes to plans and contingency plans". This was routine in all observed handoffs and
  was not a formal procedure. Nuclear plants, railroad dispatch and ambulance dispatch did not do it [O1].
- Advice. A NASA paper on the Mars rovers asks the sender to hand over problems, hypotheses and intent, and not only a
  list of actions done [O8].
- In the process industries and on the railway the reason for a state is not a fixed item. The guidance speaks of
  "task-relevant information" [O4].

### The form

- Measured, by observation. All observed handoffs in the four settings were spoken, face to face and with questions. The
  observers saw handoffs of 10 minutes in mission control, 10 to 15 in nuclear plants, 5 to 10 in railroad dispatch
  [O1].
- Advice. HSE: face to face, two-way, with speech and writing together, and with as much time as needed. A handover on a
  12-hour shift can take up to 30 minutes [O3][O4].
- Mission control: the outgoing controller writes a one-paragraph summary in the log before the handoff. The two
  controllers then work side by side for one hour [O1].
- Nuclear plants: the incoming operator reads the logs and "walks the board" first [O1][O7].

### The check

- HSE names three parts: the outgoing shift prepares, the two shifts exchange, and the incoming shift cross-checks the
  information while it takes over [O4].
- Measured, by observation. Read-back was not seen in any handoff in the four settings. A fixed order of topics was not
  seen in any setting [O1].
- Measured, by observation. The incoming person asks. In 16 mission control handoffs the receivers asked 75 questions.
  Eight of the 75 tested a statement, for example "Do you know that for sure?" [O1].
- The transfer is visible. The incoming person takes the seat or the headset. An ambulance dispatcher called it "almost
  insulting" to plug in before the handover ends [O1].
- Only mission control keeps the sender on watch after the transfer as a rule. The other three settings do it rarely
  [O1].

### The evidence

- Weak. The base is thin. Wilkinson and Lardner (2013) say that research on shift handover is "very limited". The main
  primary study is an unpublished thesis of 1992 [O3].
- Weak. Patterson and others (2004) describe what four settings do, from 422 hours of field notes made for other
  purposes. The authors did not measure if a practice works [O1].
- No trial with an outcome number was found for these industries.

### The known failures, from inquiries

- Sellafield, 1983. A failure of communication between shifts sent radioactive liquor to the sea [O4].
- Piper Alpha, 6 July 1988, 167 dead. The relief valve of pump A was off under one permit. The pump work was under a
  second permit. Suspended permits were kept in the safety office, filed by trade and not by location. Lead operators
  did not review suspended permits at the busy shift change. The night shift started pump A. Lord Cullen found that the
  handover and the permit system both failed [O4][O5].
- Texas City, 23 March 2005. The site had no rule for shift turnover. The log entries were short and unclear, and the
  next shift read them wrongly. One key instruction came by telephone and was in no log (weak route) [O6].
- Buncefield, 11 December 2005. The handover was face to face and used a log. It was still "inadequately structured,
  variable and informal". Nobody had decided which information was key. Speech was the default when people were busy.
  The wrong picture of which pipeline fed which tank lasted across more than one shift [O3].
- Balham, 20 April 2019. A rail machine moved 600 metres without authority. The RAIB told Network Rail to review
  handovers between signallers. In 2022 the regulator noted that there was still no formal structure for what the two
  signallers discuss [O9].

### What the field says does not work

- Advice. A long same-person rota can keep a mistake alive, because the same two people hand the same wrong picture back
  and forth [O3].
- Opinion. The industry looks too much at the shift-to-shift handover. Communication in the shift, and between shift and
  day staff, fails as often [O3].
- Opinion. More research and more guidance are not the gap. Sites do not apply the guidance that exists [O3].
- Opinion. Automated logs cannot replace the talk between two people. They do not show what was unexpected or what the
  plan is [O1].

## 5. A field where the receiver cannot ask: wills and advance directives

The writer of a will is dead when the will takes effect. The writer of an advance directive cannot speak when the
directive takes effect. The receiver is the executor, the court, the family or the physician.

### What crosses

- A will carries the dispositions: who gets what, and who is the executor. The reasons are usually not in the will.
- Advice. A separate letter of wishes can carry the reasons and the guidance. The letter does not bind the executor. A
  vague letter can make a gift fail [F7].
- An advance directive carries treatment choices for named situations, or values, or the name of a person who will
  decide (a proxy) [F3][F4].

### The form

- A fixed formal document, in writing, signed and witnessed. No period exists where both parties are present after the
  document takes effect.

### The check

- No check with the sender is possible afterwards. The field moves the check to three other places.
- Before: the formalities force the writer to state the words with care. The trials of advance directives added a talk
  between patient and surrogate before the event [F3].
- After, by a third party: a court reads the document. English law since 2014 reads a will as it reads a contract, from
  the words, the document as a whole and the facts at the time. Evidence of what the writer privately intended is
  admitted only where the words are meaningless or ambiguous [F5][F6].
- By repair: a court can correct a clerical error. In Marley v Rawlings a husband and a wife each signed the other's
  will, and the court rectified the will [F6].

### The evidence

- Measured. Shalowitz 2006, a review of 16 studies with 2,595 pairs of patient and surrogate and 151 scenarios.
  Surrogates predicted the treatment choice of the patient with 68% accuracy. It did not help that the patient chose the
  surrogate. It did not help that the two had talked about the choices before [F1].
- Measured. Ditto 2001, a randomised trial with 401 older outpatients and their surrogates. No form of directive, with
  or without a talk, improved the accuracy of the surrogate. The talk raised the confidence of the surrogate (summary
  only) [F3].
- Measured. SUPPORT 1995, 9,105 seriously ill patients. Before the intervention, 47% of physicians knew that their
  patient did not want resuscitation. The intervention arm (2,652 patients) showed no improvement in care or outcomes
  [F2].
- No number was found for how often an executor reads a will as the writer meant it.

### The known failures

- Opinion, on measured studies. Fagerlin and Schneider 2004: people cannot predict what they will want in a situation
  that they have never met. Families argue about whether the document applies to the case. The authors say the
  conditions for a working living will "are unmet and largely unmeetable" [F4].
- A talk before the event raises confidence without a rise in accuracy [F3].
- A letter that is clear to the writer is vague to a court [F7].

### What this field does differently

- It gives up the recovery of the private purpose. It replaces the purpose with a public rule for reading the words
  [F5][F6].
- It moves from instructions to a trusted person. Fagerlin and Schneider ask for powers of attorney in place of living
  wills [F4].
- It separates the binding text from the guidance, and gives the receiver discretion on the guidance [F7].
- It keeps a repair path for a plain error [F6].

## Across the fields

### What all of them share

1. A live exchange between two people, where one is possible, with questions allowed.
2. Speech and writing together. Each covers the gaps of the other.
3. A list of topics that somebody decided before the handover.
4. Preparation by both sides: the sender writes first, the receiver reads or looks first.
5. An active receiver. The receiver repeats, restates or plans in front of the sender.
6. A visible moment where responsibility passes: a statement, a seat, a headset, a form.
7. A check after the transfer: an overlap, a review by the sender or a cross-check.
8. Extra care for named high-risk cases: new staff, long absence, an upset in progress, work that spans the shift
   change, an override of a safety system [O3].

### Where they disagree

- Repeat the words, or restate the meaning. Aviation wants the exact words. The military and I-PASS want the receiver's
  own words. The four shift settings in [O1] use neither and rely on questions.
- A fixed order of items. Medicine, the controller relief and the military order fix the order. No setting in [O1] did.
- Where the purpose sits. The military gives the purpose a paragraph and puts it first. I-PASS and mission control carry
  it inside "contingency" and "stance". Clearances, permits and logs do not carry it. A will leaves it out of the
  binding text on purpose.
- Instructions, or a trusted agent. Aviation keeps exact instructions. The military and the literature on advance
  directives move to intent and to a person with discretion.
- A mandate to standardise. The Joint Commission mandates. Cohen and Hilligoss say the evidence does not support the
  mandate.
- The length of the overlap. Mission control uses one hour. Nuclear plants and dispatch centres use almost none. A
  military relief uses days.
- A fresh receiver, or a familiar one. A familiar pair shares a picture faster. The same pair also keeps a shared
  mistake alive [O3]. A fresh receiver finds more fixation errors [O1].

### What rests on measurement and what on tradition

Measurement supports these:

- A bundle of tool, training and observation reduced errors in one large before-and-after study [M1]. One randomised
  trial of the same tool found no effect on harm [M4].
- Read-back finds errors in single messages: 3.5% of laboratory calls [M16], and under 1% of controller messages [A3].
- Long messages fail more often than short messages [A3].
- The first minutes after a transfer carry the most errors [A6].
- Senders overrate their handoffs [M12]. A talk raises confidence more than accuracy [F3].
- Intent, written by the doctrine, matched in 34% of new cases one level down [W1].
- Surrogates match the choice of the patient in 68% of scenarios [F1].

Tradition, doctrine or inquiry findings support these, with no outcome number found:

- The back-brief, the confirmation brief and the rehearsal.
- The relief in place with a shared period.
- The one-hour overlap and the "stance" in mission control.
- The face-to-face, two-way, written-and-spoken rule in the process industries.
- The bedside handover (the reviews disagree).
- The crew briefing and its order.
- The letter of wishes.

### What none of them solves

- No routine test shows that the purpose arrived. Read-back tests the words. A checklist tests that an item was said.
  The one study that tested receivers on new cases found 34% [W1]. No field runs that test as a normal part of a
  handover.
- The sender cannot see the failure. The sender's confidence is not evidence [M12][F3].
- The check decays first. Receivers skip the synthesis when nobody watches [M7]. Read-back appears in 17% of hospital
  handoffs [M13]. A missing read-back is 25% of reported radio errors [A4].
- Compliance with the form does not prove a result. Buncefield had face-to-face handovers and logs [O3]. The Argentine
  trial raised compliance and harm stayed the same [M4].
- A wrong picture that both sides share passes every check [O3]. So does a correct read-back of a wrong instruction that
  the sender does not hear [A4].
- Instructions do not cover the case that nobody foresaw. This is the reason for intent in the military and the reason
  that living wills fail [W1][F4].
- When the sender is gone, no field recovers the purpose. The field replaces it with a rule of reading or with a trusted
  person, and accepts the loss [F1][F5].
- The evidence is weak in most fields. Designs are before-and-after, outcomes are often self-reported, publication bias
  is measured in medicine [M11], and the process industries have almost no primary research [O3].
